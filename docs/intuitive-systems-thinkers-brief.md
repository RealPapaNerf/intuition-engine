# Intuitive Systems Thinkers: pull it apart, break it, fix it

The third track of the framework, opened 2026-10-26
(`decisions/2026-10-26-pull-it-apart.md`, which binds the build and wins
where this brief and it disagree). Same engine as the other two —
predict, test, see why, iterate; a spiral syllabus; scores that are
secretly the real quantity — pointed at the thing every subject is made
of: systems. What is this part for? What depends on what? What happens
when it breaks, or there are two, or it's blocked? Why did that fail,
and is the part that failed the part that's wrong?

## Why

- **Every subject is a system, and almost no one is taught to take one
  apart.** A mechanic, a doctor, an IT tech, an engineer and an
  ecologist share one move: change one thing, predict what the whole
  does, check. Schools teach the parts lists (name the organs, label the
  circuit) and almost never the move.
- **Knockouts are how function is actually discovered.** Biologists
  learn what a gene does by knocking it out; engineers run failure
  modes and effects; doctors read a patient as a system missing
  something. A kid who has pulled the kidney out of a model and watched
  the waste climb — slowly, for a day and a half — knows what a kidney
  is for, and knows the difference between a part that matters now and
  one that matters later.
- **It is the anti-pencil-whip skill aimed at causes.** An AI will say
  "replace the LED." A kid who has burnt the replacement because the
  resistor was still a plain wire asks *why did it blow?* before
  accepting any fix — from a person, a feed, or a machine.
- **Systems intuition is measurably poor in adults.** Well-educated
  grown-ups assume a stock follows the shape of its flow (Sweeney &
  Sterman, 2000; Cronin, Gonzalez & Sterman, 2009). Practising early,
  on bathtubs and kidneys, is the bet.
- **It is the transfer probe built in.** One set of laws runs every
  world here: the bathtub's in = out is the kidney's waste, the battery's
  charge and the engine's heat. Whether a kid who learned it in one
  world recognizes it in the next is exactly the philosophy's near-
  transfer question, asked inside one pack.

## Who it's for

The same K–8 spiral. A five-year-old pulls the battery and sees the
light go out; a nine-year-old predicts how long the car runs without its
alternator, in minutes, with a band; a thirteen-year-old finds that the
steady level is inflow ÷ clearance in the kidney, the bathtub and the
drug in their own blood, and meets C = G/K as an old friend.

## The one non-negotiable principle

**Say what will happen before you see it.** Every change — out, bad,
another, moved, blocked — opens the gate, and nothing runs until the
kid has answered. Prediction is what turns a sandbox into a lesson
(Brod, Hasselhorn & Bunge, 2018; Alfieri et al., 2011: unassisted
discovery underperforms, guided discovery with feedback and elicited
explanation does well).

## The honest-physics problem, and its answer

A system "model" could easily be a lookup table of what happens when
each part comes out — which would be canned outcomes and the exact
credulity this framework exists to prevent. The answer is three laws and
a solver (the decision, §2): stuff flows between levels (conduct), one
way past a drop (oneway), or a machine turns inputs into outputs
(react). Every outcome is computed, conserved, and the same every time.

The second half of honesty — is the model true of the *real* kidney? —
cannot be guaranteed by an engine. So every system names what it models,
cites where its numbers come from, says plainly what it leaves out, and
is read by an adult (its answer key, every change run) before a kid
plays it. "Every model is wrong; some are useful" is itself a lesson,
and a kid who catches the model's edge has done real science.

## The core loop, translated

1. **Look** — the system working: the light shines, the blood stays
   clean, the car gets to grandma's. The invisible is drawn: charge,
   waste, heat, building blocks.
2. **Intervene** — take a part out, put a bad one in, add another, move
   it, block it.
3. **Predict** — will it still work? more or less? which part stops
   first? what is that part's job? how much? when?
4. **Test** — the solver runs; the cascade is said as it happens,
   slowing where it goes wrong.
5. **See why** — the system as it ships beside the system as it is now,
   and the chain of what stopped and why.
6. **Fix** — put a good one in. Did that fix it, or only what it broke?

## The age ladder (one game, three depths)

| Band | Mode | What the kid does |
|---|---|---|
| K–2 | **Feel it** | Out or in, works or not, more or less, which stops first, what's it for. Tap-to-pick, read aloud. |
| 3–5 | **Measure it** | Bands in units: how high, how much, how long. The before/after chart. The Consultant's numbers to check. |
| 6–8 | **Model it** | Find the rule: steady level = in ÷ out-per-level; series adds, parallel splits; doubling time = ln 2 ÷ rate. Name the patterns — domino, loop, spare, buffer, bottleneck — after they have been felt. Read the model's notes and find its edges. |

## The syllabus: ten units, and the edge of the model

| # | Unit | In the game | At the table | Hidden math |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Parts and jobs** | Pull each part; say what it was for | Take apart a flashlight | Function from absence |
| 2 | **Loops and chains** | A circuit is a loop: cut any piece and it goes dark. Two switches side by side (OR) or in line (AND) | A battery, a bulb and a wire | Series, parallel, Boolean AND/OR |
| 3 | **Stocks and flows** | The bathtub: where does it settle? | A jug with a hole, filled from a tap | Accumulation; steady state where in = out |
| 4 | **Delays and buffers** | Pull the alternator: the car runs an hour on the battery | A bucket with a hole feeding a plant | Time to empty = stock ÷ net outflow |
| 5 | **Spares** | One kidney is enough; two pumps cover one weak one | Two straws in one cup | Redundancy; parallel paths |
| 6 | **Holding steady** | The thermostat; the kidney letting out water above normal | A toilet cistern's float valve | Balancing feedback, setpoints, hysteresis |
| 7 | **Running away** | The virus: a recipe that copies itself, until the cell runs out | Yeast and sugar in a bottle, a balloon on top | Reinforcing loops; doubling time; the limit that bends the curve |
| 8 | **Bottlenecks** | A second fuel pump changes nothing — until the first is weak | A sibling assembly line making sandwiches | The slowest step sets the pace |
| 9 | **Root causes and cascades** | The burnt LED that burns again | The "five whys" on a real breakdown | Causal chains; fix why, not what |
| 10 | **Troubleshooting** (capstone) | Something's wrong: three checks, then swap | The mystery box | Information per check; half-splitting (the scale's bisection, again) |
| — | **Models and their limits** | Read "About this model"; find where it's wrong | Compare the model's number with a real one | Every model leaves something out |

## The worlds

The owner's own list is the first map: a whole car, an RC car, an
airplane, a logistics system, a computer, a tiny PCB, the kidney, the
cytoplasm, a virus. Built so far: the flashlight's circuit board, the
kidneys, a flu virus in a cell, the car, and the bathtub. Expressible
today: a logistics line, the heart and lungs, a pond. Waiting for new
laws (with their own decision and hand calculations): anything with
motors, drag or lift — the RC car, the airplane, a computer's power
(decision §12).

A world is not a skin. A kidney and a bathtub are different systems with
different numbers, not one system in two costumes; what is compared
across kids is the unit (did they predict the steady level?), not the
numbers. Costumes for a single system (the same circuit as a water park)
remain possible later and would follow the skin rules exactly.

## Goal archetypes

Level skeletons, stated as outcomes, scored on the goal and never the
method:

- **What is it for?** — the knockout: predict, pull, see.
- **Two of them** — add another: more, less, or nothing?
- **In its place** — put this where that was: beside it, in line with it.
- **Blocked** — one flow stopped: which parts notice?
- **Keep it alive** — hold a level in range with what you're given
  (dialysis for a body with no kidneys).
- **Find the bottleneck** — which upgrade changes anything?
- **Fix it** — something's wrong: the fewest checks, the right part.
- **Build it** (later) — compose a system from parts to meet a goal.

## The Consultant's error taxonomy

The decision's §6, in short: forgetting the spare; forgetting that a
store empties (or fills); the wrong direction; "nothing changes" about a
part wired straight to the gauge; a stock that "settles" with no way
out; a number where there can be none; ten times, or a tenth; and plain
confident nonsense. Every one is caught by looking at the system, never
by how the sentence sounds.

## The AI's role

- **The coach** asks: *What is it running on? Where does that go when
  nothing takes it out? Which part does the same job? What did you change
  — and which change made the difference?* It never says what a part is
  for.
- **The Consultant** answers, and is wrong one time in five, in the ways
  above.
- **Composing systems** from a sentence is where a live model earns its
  place in this pack — and it composes behind the strictest gate in the
  repo: a model can obey every law and still teach the wrong kidney, so
  an adult reads its answer key before any kid plays it (decision §8).

## The kitchen table

Phase 0 is a teardown table. Rules first, because real things bite:

- **No mains electricity, ever.** No opening anything that plugs into the
  wall: microwaves and old TVs hold a charge that kills after they're
  unplugged.
- **No lithium batteries opened, bent or punctured.** AA and AAA cells,
  a 3 V coin cell, or a 9 V for the breadboard, with a grown-up.
- **Eye protection when prying; small parts away from under-threes.**
  Nothing on a real car without a grown-up, engine off and cold.

Then: take apart a dead flashlight and a thrift-store toy; build the
flashlight's circuit on a breadboard (with the resistor — and, with a
grown-up, see a cheap LED dim when the resistor is too big); fill a jug
with a hole from a tap and predict where the water settles; a coffee
filter and food colouring for what a kidney's filter keeps and lets go;
yeast in a bottle under a balloon for growth that runs away and stops.

**The "fair test" card.** Change one thing at a time, and say which thing
before you change it. Research says this has to be taught, not just
prompted — probe questions alone did not teach it to 7–10-year-olds;
instruction plus probes did, and it transferred (Chen & Klahr, 1999).
So a grown-up teaches it at the table, and the game's debrief asks the
probe: *what were you trying to find out?*

**The mystery box.** A grown-up breaks one thing in a flashlight or a
toy (a flat cell, a reversed cell, a gap in the spring, a tiny bit of
tape on a contact). The kid finds it with the fewest checks — and says,
before each check, what it will show.

## What "intuition" means here, measurably

- **Calibration** on works / more-or-less / how much / when, per unit,
  over months — the same prestige stat as every pack.
- **Cascade reasoning**: how often "which stops first" and "will that
  one keep working" are right, especially two connections away (the
  indirect effects young reasoners miss; Grotzer).
- **Stock-and-flow probes**: bathtub tasks in the style of Sweeney &
  Sterman, before a season and after.
- **Planted-error catch rate** against the Consultant, by error kind.
- **Troubleshooting**: mysteries solved, and checks used against the
  fewest that would have done.
- **Transfer**: a kid who learned C = G/K on the kidneys, given the
  bathtub or a battery cold — faster than a matched kid?
- **Table proof**: the mystery box at the kitchen table tracks the
  game's mysteries.

## Build plan

- **Phase 0** — the teardown table, the fair-test card, the mystery box.
  Playtest on the owner's own kids.
- **Phase 1** (built, 2026-10-26) — the three laws and the solver; the
  bench (out, bad, good, another, beside / in line, blocked); the
  questions made from the system; the Consultant; something's wrong;
  five systems pinned to hand calculations; Pull It Apart; the
  Workbench.
- **Phase 2** — couplings and square laws, each with its own decision
  and hand calculations; the RC car, the airplane, the computer, a
  logistics line.
- **Phase 3** — the coach composing systems onto the Workbench for
  review; settings for this pack (a system plus the lesson's focus).
- **Phase 4** — compare questions and cross-system analogies; the
  patterns named at model depth; design mode (build a system to a goal).

## Open questions

- How much reading the Workbench can assume of a parent; whether a
  visual editor (drag parts, draw wires) must come before most parents
  can put a system in without an AI chat.
- Whether "which stops first" should accept every part that stops at
  the same instant (it does now) or ask the kid to order them.
- Whether "the same" should be 2% for every gauge, or per gauge.
- How the debrief, the explain prompt and the postmortem share one
  result without becoming a toll at the kitchen table — one at a time
  now; Stage 0 decides.
- Whether a world should carry a costume of its own (the circuit as a
  water park), and whether that helps the transfer probe or muddies it.

## Done means

- A kid asks "what's that part for?" about something at home, and then
  says what would happen without it — and is usually right.
- A kid who is told "just replace it" asks why it broke.
- A ten-year-old says "it'll settle where in equals out" about a bath, a
  bank account or a fever, unprompted.
- An eighth grader troubleshoots a broken thing with a plan — which check
  first, and why — instead of swapping parts at random.
