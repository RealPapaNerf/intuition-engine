# Intuitive Negotiators: the communication track

The second track of the framework planned in
`intuitive-engineers-brief.md`. Same engine — predict, test, see why,
iterate under constraint; spiral syllabus; tailored difficulty; scores
that are secretly the real quantity — pointed at a different subject:
reading people, negotiating, having hard conversations, and leading.

Not a fork. The pedagogy engine, the calibration scoring, the
coach/Consultant pair, and the age ladder are shared. The tracks even
cross: the leadership units run *inside* the engineering track's team
contests, because leading is learned while building something, not in
the abstract.

## Why

- Schools teach almost none of this, and it is the half of life that
  AI changes least. As AI gets better at producing answers, the people
  who do best will be the ones who can leverage AI *and* form real
  relationships: read a room, earn trust, say the hard thing kindly,
  and get a team moving. Those are trainable skills, and almost nobody
  trains them before adulthood — and then only in airport books.
- The same pencil-whip failure exists socially: kids who never practice
  hard conversations grow into adults who avoid them, delegate them to
  a text message, or let an AI draft something technically polite that
  lands terribly.
- The source material already exists and is adult-shaped; the work is
  translating it into games a kid plays before they can spell
  "empathy."

## Source material, and what each contributes

| Source | What it becomes in the game |
|---|---|
| Chris Voss, *Never Split the Difference* | The move set: tactical empathy, mirroring, labeling, calibrated questions, the accusation audit, "that's right" as the win condition |
| Robert Cialdini, *Influence* | The defense curriculum: recognize the seven levers (reciprocity, commitment, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity, unity) when they're being used **on you** |
| Jocko Willink, *Extreme Ownership* | The leadership units: ownership, the after-action debrief, decentralized command, leading up and down |
| Carol Dweck, *Mindset* | Not a unit — the scoring philosophy of the whole framework: praise is always for process and iteration, never talent; every stat is shown as a growth curve; failure states read "not yet" |

## The honest-physics problem, and its answer

The engineering track's credibility rests on real solvers behind the
cartoon. People don't have solvers. The honest equivalent is a
**hidden-state character**: every character in every scenario carries a
consistent hidden brief — what they say they want (their *position*),
what they actually want (their *interest*), what they're afraid of, and
a trust meter that moves by fixed, discoverable rules (interrupting
drops it; labeling a feeling correctly raises it; a correct label of
their *hidden* worry raises it a lot).

Honest here means *consistent and discoverable*, never arbitrary. The
kid's intuition is calibrated against the same hidden state every
replay, the way the frog game calibrates against the same beam model.
Scripted state machines first; later, an AI plays the character while
the hidden brief and the trust rules stay authored (see below).

## The core loop, translated

1. **Look** — read the situation: the character's face, words, posture.
2. **Predict** — commit before speaking: What do they really want?
   How will they react if I say what I'm about to say?
3. **Test** — say it (pick the line, or later, speak/type your own).
4. **See why** — the replay shows the trust meter over the whole
   conversation and marks the exact line where it turned. The social
   slow-motion break.
5. **Iterate** — same character, same hidden brief, new approach.

The calibration score transfers directly: predicting a person's
reaction is the social version of predicting the branch breaking, and
**tactical empathy is literally prediction of another mind** — which is
why this is one framework and not two apps.

## The win condition inversion

The game never scores "did you beat them." Victory is understanding:

- The **"that's right" moment** — the character confirms your label of
  their hidden interest — is the victory screen.
- Deals score by how well **both** sides' hidden interests are met.
  Extracting value from someone who didn't understand the deal scores
  *negative* once their hidden state reacts (they don't trade with you
  next level; word spreads to other characters).
- Guardrail, stated as a hard rule: this track must never become
  manipulation training. The Cialdini levers are only ever scored as
  *recognition* — spotting them used on you — never as weapons to use
  on others. Any manipulative line the kid finds works once and then
  visibly poisons the trust meter for the rest of the season. Empathy
  first is not a tone choice; it's the scoring function.

## The age ladder

| Band | Mode | What the kid does |
|---|---|---|
| K–2 | **Feel it** | Name feelings from faces and voices; take turns; "guess what your sister wants"; notice that people want things they don't say |
| 3–5 | **Measure it** | The moves get names: mirror, label, trade. Positions vs interests. The trust meter becomes visible. First scripted hard conversations (apologize, say no, ask for something) |
| 6–8 | **Model it** | The full toolkit, named: accusation audit, calibrated questions, anchoring awareness, walk-away thinking. Multi-party negotiations. Leading a team and running its debrief |

## The syllabus: nine units

| # | Unit | Game | Hidden lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reading people | Guess the feeling: faces, voices, body language; predict what the character does next | Emotions have tells; reading them is a skill, not a gift |
| 2 | Listening as a superpower | Make the character open up using only mirrors and labels; talking about yourself closes them | Mirroring, labeling; listening gathers the information that wins later units |
| 3 | Wants under wants | The orange: two kids, one orange — one needs the peel, one the juice. First hidden briefs | Positions vs interests; "why" beats "what" |
| 4 | Trades | Trading village: multi-item trades where value differs by person; reciprocity tracked | Both sides can win; value is subjective; favors come back |
| 5 | Hard conversations | The ladder: apologize, say no kindly, ask for what you want, tell a friend they hurt you, deliver bad news. Rehearse, replay, retry | The accusation audit ("say their worry before they do"); scary things said early cost less |
| 6 | The tricks | Spot the lever: ads, peer pressure, and salesy characters; name which of Cialdini's seven levers is being pulled before choosing | Influence literacy as defense; recognizing the pull removes its power |
| 7 | Calibrated questions | The dragon that only hears questions: you cannot state, demand, or argue — only ask how and what | "How am I supposed to do that?" moves mountains; questions make the other side solve your problem |
| 8 | Leading the build | Joint missions in the engineering track's Shop: the leader may not touch the pieces — only talk. Then the debrief: the leader narrates what went wrong owning all of it | Extreme ownership; decentralized command; a debrief without blame gets the truth |
| 9 | The big negotiation (capstone) | Multi-party, every character with a hidden brief, time pressure — then the real thing: negotiate an actual family decision (trip, pet, bedtime) under agreed rules | Everything at once, with real stakes and a real counterpart: a parent |

## The AI's role here

- **The coach** stays question-only, as in the engineering track:
  "What do you think she's afraid of?" "What did the mirror do that
  the argument didn't?"
- **Characters** are where a live AI eventually earns its place — this
  is the one job scripts do worst and models do best. The hidden brief
  and trust rules stay authored (the honesty guarantee); the AI
  improvises the character's lines within them, so the kid can speak
  freely instead of picking from menus. Scripted state machines ship
  first; AI characters are a later phase, heavily sandboxed.
- **The Consultant** (see the engineering brief) appears here too: it
  drafts messages for you — technically polite, factually fine, and
  sometimes socially tone-deaf. The kid's job: spot *why this will make
  your sister dig in* before sending. Same trust-calibration stat, same
  lesson: an AI draft is a hypothesis until you've read it as the
  receiver.

## What "intuition" means here, measurably

- **People calibration**: predicted reactions match actual reactions,
  improving over time — the social twin of the prediction meter.
- **Hidden-interest discovery rate**: how often the kid uncovers the
  real want, and how fast.
- **"That's right" moments** per conversation.
- **De-escalations**: conversations that were heading down and turned.
- **Transfer, parent-logged**: the kid labeled a sibling's feeling
  mid-argument; asked a calibrated question instead of saying no; had
  the hard conversation they were avoiding.

## Phase 0 is the dinner table

Like the engineering track's spaghetti-and-washers rule: if the loop
doesn't work with zero technology, stop. Voss's own homework translates
to kid-size and costs nothing:

- Label one feeling at dinner, every night ("It sounds like you had a
  frustrating day"), and watch what it does.
- One real negotiation a week with actual stakes and agreed rules
  (bedtime, screen time, chores) — parent commits to being movable by
  good technique.
- A card deck of hidden briefs for two-player table play: one card says
  what you ask for, and below it, what you actually want.

## Build plan

- **Phase 0** — the dinner-table protocol and the hidden-brief card
  deck, printable. Playtest on my own kids.
- **Phase 1** — scripted scenarios as a small web app (same habits:
  browser, offline-capable, no accounts): units 1–3, trust-meter
  replay, the predict gate.
- **Phase 2** — the full unit ladder, hard-conversation rehearsals,
  Spot the Lever.
- **Phase 3** — AI-played characters inside authored briefs; the
  Consultant's tone-deaf drafts; per-kid tailoring.
- **Phase 4** — joint missions with the engineering track (unit 8),
  season/contest mode, the capstone.

## Open questions

- How a kid inputs their side early on: menus of lines (safe, limited)
  vs free speech/typing (needs AI understanding much sooner).
- How much of this works solo vs needing a second human; which units
  require the parent.
- Whether faces/voices should be realistic or stylized for the K–2
  band (reading real faces is the skill; stylized is easier to start).
- Privacy line for AI characters: conversations are practice, not a
  diary — what is stored, and the answer should be close to nothing.

## Done means

- A kid labels a sibling's feeling, unprompted, in the middle of an
  argument — and it works.
- A kid asks "how am I supposed to do that?" instead of saying no.
- An 8th grader runs a post-contest debrief and owns the failure
  without being asked to.
- A kid reads an AI-drafted message and says "this will make it worse"
  — and can say why.
